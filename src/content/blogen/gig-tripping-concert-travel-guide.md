**Short answer:** Gig-tripping means buying your concert ticket for a show in another country, then building a city break around it. It sounds extravagant. It is often the cheaper option. Face-value tickets in Southern and Eastern Europe frequently cost a fraction of US or UK resale prices for the same tour. Add a flight and two hotel nights and the total can still undercut a single secondary-market ticket at home. Plus you get a trip out of it.

Your favorite artist announces a tour. You join the queue the second presale opens. Position 24,000. An hour later, everything is gone, and resale prices sit at triple face value.

This is the moment more and more fans flip the logic. They open the full tour schedule instead. Same band, same production, three weeks later in Madrid or Prague. Tickets there: available, official, cheaper.

## What is gig-tripping and why is everyone doing it?

Gig-tripping means the concert is the reason for the trip, not a side activity. According to a survey by insurer Generali, about 60 percent of music fans are open to flying internationally if it means cheaper tickets.

Taylor Swift's Eras Tour made the math famous. At the Paris La Défense Arena, roughly 9,000 of the 40,000 seats were filled by Americans. CNN ran the numbers at the time: flights to Paris, two nights in a four-star hotel and a ticket came to about $1,740 in total. That was still $760 less than the cheapest available ticket for the Miami shows.

Then came the Oasis reunion. During the UK onsale, dynamic pricing pushed standing tickets from £150 to over £400 while fans sat in the queue, and the UK's competition watchdog opened an investigation into Ticketmaster. Plenty of fans drew their own conclusion and booked shows in other countries instead.

One thing this guide is not: a festival guide. Multi-day festivals follow their own logic, covered in our [festival travel guide](/en/blog/festival-travel-guide/). This is about single concerts, the big stadium and arena tours. Wikipedia's entry on the [concert tour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concert_tour) sums up the key fact: same artist, same production, a long list of cities. The prices change dramatically by country.

## Why are concert tickets so much cheaper abroad?

Three forces drive the gap.

**Purchasing power and local pricing.** Promoters price tickets country by country. Base prices in Southern and Eastern Europe sit well below the US and UK. On the Eras Tour, entry-level tickets started at $312 in Stockholm, $324 in Spain and $340 in Paris. Resale entry for the Liverpool shows hovered around £540.

**Resale laws.** France bans ticket resale without the promoter's approval. Ireland and Portugal ban resale above face value. That keeps secondary prices low in those markets. The US and UK have no comparable hard caps, so prices for hot shows explode.

**Dynamic pricing.** Dynamic pricing in the primary sale is standard practice in the US and common in the UK. Many EU promoters use it rarely or not at all. Same tour, different onsale, very different price.

For US and UK fans, that means one habit change: read the whole tour schedule, not just your nearest date. A 2026 example: Metallica's M72 European leg runs from Athens in early May to London in early July, with stops including Budapest, Frankfurt and Dublin. Miss out at home and you still have a continent of options.

## The math: what does a concert trip really cost?

Take a typical scenario. Stadium show, your local dates sold out, resale standing tickets at $600 to $900.

The abroad option, say Madrid or Prague:

- Official face-value ticket: $80 to $150
- Round-trip flight, booked early: $350 to $600 from the US East Coast, far less from the UK
- Two mid-range hotel nights: $150 to $250
- Transit and food: $100 to $150

That lands between $680 and $1,150. Now compare fairly. The resale ticket at home costs up to $900, and all you get for it is one evening. The trip gets you the same show plus a weekend in a new city. From the UK, with $60 round-trip fares to half of Europe, the case is even easier.

The travel cost is the part you control. The ground rules are in our guide on [when to book flights](/en/blog/when-to-book-flights/): book the day the tour is announced, not three weeks before the show when thousands of fans hunt the same route.

## Where do you buy tickets for shows abroad safely?

Ticket buying is the risky part. Work in this order:

**Official channels first.** Every tour lists its official sellers per country on the artist's site. Register for presales in every country you would fly to.

**Then the regulated resale market.** Once onsales are over, use established marketplaces with buyer protection instead of forums or social media deals. Platforms like [TicketNetwork](https://www.ticketnetwork.com/) let you compare prices for the same tour across many cities and countries at once. That comparison is the whole point of gig-tripping.

**Scams follow three patterns.** One: payment by bank transfer or gift card only, with no buyer protection. Two: ticket screenshots offered as proof, which are worthless since most tours only enable transfers shortly before the show. Three: prices far below market for a sold-out date. Nobody gives away hot tickets.

Also check the entry rules for that country's show. Some events are personalized, meaning the name on the ticket must match your ID. That information lives in the promoter's FAQ.

## When is the concert trip worth it?

The decision grid, kept deliberately simple:

| Situation | Worth the trip? |
|---|---|
| Home show available at face value | No, just buy at home |
| Home show sold out, resale under 150 percent of face | Probably not, unless you wanted the trip anyway |
| Resale at home over 200 percent, official tickets abroad available | Yes, run the numbers and book |
| No show scheduled in your country | Yes, the trip is the only way in |
| A city you already wanted to visit is on the routing | Yes, show plus dream city is the best combo |

Three honest caveats. First: always compare totals, not tickets. Second: build in a buffer day, because a cancelled flight on show day sinks the whole plan. Third: postponements happen, so book flights with rebooking options and rooms with free cancellation wherever possible.

## How to plan your gig trip in five steps

1. **Read the entire tour schedule.** Every city, every country, every onsale date. Not just the dates near you.
2. **Shortlist two or three host cities.** Combine ticket availability with places you actually want to see.
3. **Ticket first, travel immediately after.** Book the flight the same day you secure the ticket. A quick comparison on [Aviasales](https://www.aviasales.com/) shows which of your shortlisted cities is cheapest to reach on your dates.
4. **Pick your base with the venue in mind.** Stadiums often sit outside the center, and what matters is the ride back after the encore. For two of Europe's biggest concert cities we have neighborhood guides: [where to stay in London](/en/blog/where-to-stay-london/) and [where to stay in Madrid](/en/blog/where-to-stay-madrid/).
5. **Keep the day after free.** The best part of a gig trip is the slow city day after the show, when everyone else is flying home.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How far in advance should you book flights for a concert trip?

Ideally the same day you buy the ticket, which usually means several months out. Once a show sells out, fares into that city climb fast because thousands of fans search the same dates.

### What happens if the show gets postponed?

Your ticket normally stays valid for the new date. Your travel does not. That is why you book flights with rebooking options, choose rooms with free cancellation and lock in as little as possible before the date feels solid. Standard travel insurance rarely covers a canceled concert, so read the policy wording before you rely on it.

### Which countries have the cheapest concert tickets in Europe?

Broadly, Southern and Eastern Europe: Poland, Czechia, Hungary, Portugal and Spain usually sit below Germany on base prices and far below the UK. Strict resale laws in France, Ireland and Portugal also keep secondary prices low there. Exact numbers vary by tour, so always compare the specific show.

### How do you spot ticket scams before you pay?

By the payment method and the price. Legitimate platforms offer buyer protection and deliver tickets through official transfer systems. Red flags: prepayment by wire transfer, screenshots offered as proof of authenticity, and prices far below market for sold-out dates.
